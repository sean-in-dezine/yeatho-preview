# Microgreens in Central Texas: Fast Food from Small Space

Microgreens are the cotyledon and first true leaves of a plant, harvested 7–21 days after sowing. They are dense in the nutrients the seed stored for early growth. In Central Texas they are practical because they can be grown indoors year-round, outside the heat, drought, and freeze cycles that limit outdoor production.

A spare room, garage corner, or small hoop structure with consistent temperature (roughly 65–75°F) and 12–16 hours of light produces usable trays on a weekly cycle. Soil, coconut coir, or simple hydroponic setups all work. Many Central Texas growers run them with no pesticides and harvest to order so the product never sits. Delivery radius is often limited (Austin metro, Bastrop, surrounding counties) because the crop is perishable and the point is freshness.

This is not a claim that microgreens replace a full garden or a pasture. It is a statement that a high-density, short-cycle crop fits the same self-sufficiency logic as raw milk, fruit trees, and acreage: produce something useful on the land or in the building you already control, then move it to people who want it.

## Why it fits Texas

Outdoor growing in Central Texas contends with summer heat above 100°F, occasional freezes, hail, and water restrictions. Indoor microgreens sidestep most of that. The same operator can also run outdoor beds in shoulder seasons. Local restaurants and direct customers in Austin and surrounding counties already buy from small producers. Cottage-food and direct-to-consumer paths exist for many of these sales; wholesale to restaurants usually needs a food-handler permit. Confirm current rules with the Texas Department of Agriculture before scaling.

## Practical notes from existing operations

Growers in the Austin area already sell at farmers markets, deliver to homes, and supply restaurants. Some emphasize plastic-free systems and compost from previous cycles. Others use hydroponic trays. Both approaches produce a crop that can be harvested and delivered the same day or next day. Startup cost for a small indoor setup is low relative to a full farm; the constraint is consistent light, temperature, and a buyer who wants the product fresh.

## FAQ: Microgreens in Central Texas

**What are microgreens?**  
Young vegetable greens harvested at the cotyledon or first-true-leaf stage, typically 7–21 days after sowing. They are nutrient-dense relative to their size and are eaten fresh.

**Can microgreens be grown year-round in Texas?**  
Yes, indoors. Consistent temperature and artificial light remove the outdoor heat, drought, and freeze constraints of Central Texas.

**What setup is required?**  
Trays, growing medium (soil, coir, or hydroponic), seed, light (LED works), and temperature control in the 65–75°F range. Many operators harvest to order so inventory does not sit.

**Are pesticides used?**  
Many small Central Texas producers run pesticide-free. That is a production choice, not a legal requirement for the crop itself.

**Where are they sold in Central Texas?**  
Farmers markets, direct home delivery, and restaurant accounts in the Austin metro and nearby counties. Several small farms already operate in the area.

**Is this a cottage-food product?**  
Direct-to-consumer sales at markets or to private customers often fall under cottage-food rules in Texas. Restaurant wholesale typically requires a food-handler permit. Verify current requirements before selling.

**How does this connect to other self-sufficiency work?**  
It is a short-cycle, high-density crop that can be produced on small acreage or indoors. It sits next to raw milk, fruit trees, and rural property work as one more way to produce food from land or buildings already controlled.

**Does yeatho sell microgreens?**  
Not currently as a public product. The writing is about the crop and its fit with Central Texas self-sufficiency.

**What varieties are common?**  
Radish, sunflower, pea shoots, mustard, and various brassicas appear frequently. Operators often rotate or grow to order based on restaurant and market demand.

**How fast is the cycle?**  
Most trays are ready in 7–21 days depending on variety and conditions. Weekly or twice-weekly harvests are common for small producers.

Microgreens are one more lane: fast, dense, and controllable even when the outdoor weather in Central Texas is not.
