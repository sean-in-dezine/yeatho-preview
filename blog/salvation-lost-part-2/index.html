Salvation Lost, Part 2

By Sean Kincaid

What do Hebrews 6 and 10 actually warn against?

Hebrews 6:4–6 describes people who have been enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, shared in the Holy Spirit, and tasted the powers of the age to come, then fall away. It says it is impossible to restore them again to repentance. Hebrews 10:26–29 warns that if someone goes on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins—only judgment. Both passages are severe. The question is whether they describe genuine believers who lose salvation.

How does Hebrews 10 fit the finished sacrifice?

Chapters 7–10 contrast the repeated sacrifices of the old system with Christ’s once-for-all offering. By a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified, and where there is forgiveness there is no longer any offering for sin (Hebrews 10:14–18). The warning that “there no longer remains a sacrifice” presupposes that the one sacrifice has already been offered and made known. The danger is treating that finished work with contempt—trampling the Son of God, profaning the blood of the covenant, outraging the Spirit of grace—and turning back to the old pattern. It functions like the rebuke in James 2 or Romans 6: it confronts people who have heard the truth about Christ’s finished work and calls them not to set it aside. It does not say that those who have believed and been secured by the Son can lose the sacrifice already applied to them.

How does Hebrews 6 fit the unforgivable-sin pattern?

The language of being enlightened, tasting, and sharing in the Holy Spirit describes real exposure to the Spirit’s work and the gospel’s power. They have tasted and seen that the Lord is good. This is the same kind of proximity seen in the unforgivable sin: the Pharisees watched Jesus cast out a demon by the Spirit and attributed it to Beelzebul (Matthew 12:22–32). Both involve clear encounter with the Spirit’s activity followed by settled rejection. For a moment the truth that Jesus is the way is unmistakable. They then reject it. “Shared in” need not equal the permanent indwelling that belongs to those the Father has given the Son. Once the clear sight has been finally refused, it is impossible to restore them again to repentance. They cannot even be brought back to the point where that change of mind is possible.

Why don’t they confess or believe?

They have tasted and seen that the Lord is good, but they do not confess that Jesus is the Son of God or believe that God raised him from the dead. You either believe and confess, or you do not. They do not. Examples of why they stop short:

  • The cost is too high—family pressure, loss of status, or the demand to return to the old covenant system.
  • They love the present world and its advantages more than the promise of Christ.
  • They are offended by the shame of the cross or by identifying with a rejected Messiah.
  • After the moment of clarity they simply refuse to entrust themselves to him.

Whatever the reason, they do not believe and confess. Their rejection after clear sight closes the door.

Where do apostates and pretenders fit?

Genuine belief that God raised Jesus from the dead and confession of him as Lord (Romans 10:9; 1 John 4:15; John 3:16) places a person among those the Father draws and the Son secures. That person cannot later be reclassified as never known. Apostates and pretenders are people who never believed and confessed. They may have been enlightened, tasted that the Lord is good, remained in the community, claimed works, or acknowledged facts about Jesus. None of those things is believing and confessing. Their departure or barrenness reveals they were never of the flock (1 John 2:19). You either believe and confess, or you do not. There is no third option.

Does this leave any room for losing salvation?

No. Jesus said he will lose none of those the Father gives him and that no one can snatch them out of his hand (John 6:37–39; 10:28–29). “I never knew you” describes a relationship that never existed. Hebrews 6 and 10 remain real warnings against final, deliberate rejection after the truth has been clearly tasted and seen. They mark the finality of refusing the light. They do not override the security of those who believe and confess. The warnings instruct and rebuke. They do not create a path from belonging to being cut off.
