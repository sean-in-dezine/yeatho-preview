Matthew 7:21–23 — A Warning to Us, a Condemnation for Them

By Sean Kincaid

What is Jesus actually saying in Matthew 7:21–23?

Jesus describes people who will stand before him on that day and say, “Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?” He answers, “I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness.” The pre-resurrection commission and Judas supply the sharpest and clearest illustration of this judgment. The “depart from me / I do not know you” formula itself is broader and appears in other places for empty association. The larger warning and thematic implication is for us. “Many” simply means a considerable number; it does not fix the era of the claimants.

What works are these people claiming?

They claim prophecy, casting out demons, and mighty works done in Jesus’ name. Those works closely match the authority Jesus gave the Twelve and the Seventy during his earthly ministry (Matthew 10:1–8; Luke 9:1–2; 10:17). The match is partial—exorcism and healing fit directly; prophecy is not listed in those exact verses—but it is still the closest positive authorization for the cluster of works named. Judas was one of the Twelve. He received that commission. Later Jesus called him a devil and the son of perdition (John 6:70–71; 17:12). Judas is the one we know of for certain who held that authority and was never known by Jesus. He proves that such a claim without belonging was possible before the resurrection. Later apparent works are either counterfeit signs or self-deceived claims; they do not contradict the post-resurrection tie between genuine power and the Spirit.

Could Judas have believed what Romans 10:9 requires?

No. Romans 10:9–10 says that if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Judas died by suicide before the resurrection ever happened. He could not have believed in an event that had not yet occurred. This point is narrow and illustrative: it does not claim that no one before Easter had any form of faith, and it is not required for the security of believers. John already establishes Judas’s unbelief. Even the other disciples did not believe the resurrection until after they saw the risen Jesus (Luke 24:11).

Was Jesus only warning those people, or was he warning us by using them as the example?

The pre-resurrection commission and Judas give the sharpest and clearest illustration. The formula itself is broader. Genuine believers already belong to him; “I never knew you” already secures them. Matthew 7 is part of the Sermon on the Mount. All Scripture is profitable for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16). The saying instructs believers not to confuse ministry activity or public works with the main thing. That earlier setting supplies the vivid illustration; the ongoing warning calls those who do belong to keep their hearts right.

What changed after the resurrection?

Before the resurrection Jesus could (and did) authorize people, including Judas, to do those works under a temporary commission. After the resurrection he promised the Holy Spirit to those who believe and abide in him (John 14–16). The Spirit came at Pentecost. John later writes that whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God, God abides in him and he in God (1 John 4:15). Genuine exercise of Christ’s power is now tied to the presence of the Spirit. Later people who appear to do such works either perform counterfeit signs (Matthew 24:24; 2 Thessalonians 2:9) or only think they do them. This does not contradict the Spirit-tie. The pre-resurrection example remains the sharpest illustration; the thematic warning continues for believers.

Why can this literally not apply to genuine believers?

Three reasons make the identification impossible. First, the people in Matthew 7 appeal to works they performed in Jesus’ name; Jesus does not say they believed and then fell away—he says “I never knew you.” Second, after the resurrection, the content of saving faith includes believing that God raised Jesus from the dead (Romans 10:9). Genuine believers have that faith. Third, whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God has God abiding in him (1 John 4:15), and those the Father gives the Son will never be cast out or snatched away (John 6:37–39; 10:28–29). A person who has been drawn, has believed the gospel of the risen Christ, and abides already belongs to Christ and cannot be the person Jesus says he never knew. “I never knew you” already secures believers. The warning still instructs them without threatening their final standing.

Does “I never knew you” mean a true believer can be rejected?

No. Jesus says he will lose none of those the Father gives him and that no one can snatch them out of his hand (John 6:37–39, 44; 10:28–29). The wording in Matthew 7 is “I never knew you,” not “I once knew you and then stopped.” Righteousness is received by faith, not earned by a perfect record of works (Romans 3–4; 2 Corinthians 5:21). The thief on the cross believed and was promised paradise with no time left for a lifetime of good works (Luke 23:39–43). The people in Matthew 7 appeal to their works; Jesus does not say they once had saving faith and then lost it. He says the relationship was never there. Genuine believers already belong to him.

What is the warning actually for, then, if believers already belong?

The larger thematic warning is for us: get the heart right and prioritize the main thing. It is not a threat of being cut off. Think of Martha and Mary (Luke 10:38–42). Martha was distracted with much serving and anxious about many things. Mary sat at Jesus’ feet and listened to his word. Jesus said Mary had chosen the good part, which would not be taken away from her. He did not say Martha was lost; he showed that sitting at his feet is the better priority. Paul notes that some preach Christ from envy and selfish ambition, while others do so from goodwill (Philippians 1:15–18). He rejoices that Christ is preached even when the motives are wrong. Selfish ambition is not good for the preachers, but the gospel still goes forth. First Corinthians 13:2 shows the same distinction: faith that can move mountains without love is nothing. The question is not salvation; it is whether the better thing has been chosen. The warning in Matthew 7 calls believers away from treating ministry activity, public works, or status as the main thing. It urges them to sit at Jesus’ feet. It does not say that failing to do so will result in being cut off.

What about James 2? Doesn’t that prove faith without works is useless for salvation?

James is writing to people already inside the believing community (James 1:1). He challenges a profession of faith that produces no useful action and notes that even the demons believe and shudder (James 2:14–19). Even if the question “can that faith save him?” is loaded with eternal destiny, James does not answer it by making works the ground of salvation. He rebukes barren profession the way Paul urges believers to work out their salvation with fear and trembling (Philippians 2:12)—not to earn it, but to live in light of it. The comparison with demons is a rebuke meant to awaken, not a redefinition of who belongs to Christ.

What does the parable of the sower add?

In Luke 8 only the seed the devil snatches does not believe and is not saved (Luke 8:12). The rocky soil receives the word with joy and believes for a while, then falls away under testing. The thorny soil is choked by cares and riches and does not mature. Both of those soils become plants—language of life—even if they do not endure or bear full fruit (Luke 8:13–14). Real reception of the word can occur without perfect perseverance or mature fruit. Matthew 7 warns against empty claims of power; the sower shows that life can be present even where fruit is incomplete. The two do not cancel each other once “I never knew you” is kept for those who never truly believed.

How do 1 John, Romans, and the mustard seed fit?

1 John sets a high ideal: knowing God should show itself in keeping his commands, and the one born of God does not keep practicing sin (1 John 2:3–6; 3:6–9). Yet the same letter says that if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous (1 John 2:1–2). The standard is real; it is not a demand for sinless perfection as the condition of remaining saved. Paul forbids continuing in sin (Romans 6), then describes the ongoing struggle that remains inside the believer (Romans 7), and then declares there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1). When the disciples could not cast out a demon even though Jesus had already given them authority, he rebuked their little faith and said that faith the size of a mustard seed would have been enough (Matthew 17:20). Genuine trust—even a very small amount—in the power God supplies is sufficient. The power is in the one trusted, not in how large our confidence feels or how many works we have completed.

Is there a parallel with the unforgivable sin?

Yes. When the Pharisees saw Jesus cast out a demon and said he did it by Beelzebul, Jesus warned that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit would not be forgiven (Matthew 12:22–32; Mark 3:22–30). That saying was aimed at people who watched the clear work of the Spirit and deliberately called it the work of Satan. Both Matthew 7 and the unforgivable-sin saying confront people who stood in the presence of Jesus’ power and still rejected him. Both remain strong warnings that push us toward real trust and away from empty or malicious claims. Neither functions as a general rule that a struggling believer has unknowingly lost salvation.

What about people who believed because of the signs?

John 2:23–25 records that many believed in his name when they saw the signs he was doing, yet Jesus did not entrust himself to them because he knew what was in them. Pre-resurrection belief based on signs could exist without the relationship he requires. This fits the same pattern as the Matthew 7 claimants and Judas: proximity to power or temporary response is not the same as being known.

Similar warnings

Luke 13:25–27 and Matthew 25:12 use similar “depart from me” or “I do not know you” language for people whose claim is association rather than miracle-working. These show that the formula itself is broader than the commission. They support the thematic warning against empty profession. They do not cancel the sharpest illustration supplied by the pre-resurrection commission and by Judas. Jesus names no one in Matthew 7; the identification of the sharpest case is an inference from the works named.

What is the cohesive conclusion?

The pre-resurrection commission and Judas give the sharpest and clearest illustration of the prophetic judgment in Matthew 7:21–23. The “depart / I do not know you” formula itself is broader. “Many” means a considerable number. Judas proves that someone could hold that temporary authority and still be unknown to Jesus. Later apparent works are counterfeit or self-deceived and do not contradict the Spirit-tie. The larger warning and thematic implication is for us. Genuine believers already belong to him, and “I never knew you” already secures them. Jesus uses that earlier example to warn us not to confuse ministry activity, public works, or the ability to invoke his name with the main thing—knowing him and keeping the heart right. After the resurrection, genuine confession that Jesus is the Son of God means God abides in the person (1 John 4:15), and genuine exercise of his power is tied to the Spirit. The security promises stand: those the Father draws and the Son secures will never be cast out or snatched away. “I never knew you” describes a relationship that never existed. The warning to believers is like Jesus’ word about Mary sitting at his feet: choose the better part. It is like Paul’s observation that the gospel can go forth even through selfish ambition, and like the reminder that mountain-moving faith without love is nothing. It calls us to get the heart right and prioritize sitting at Jesus’ feet over secondary activity. It does not say that failing to do so will result in being cut off. James rebukes barren profession without making works the ground of salvation. The sower, 1 John, and Romans call believers to a living response and set the ideal high, while still providing the advocate and the assurance of no condemnation. Fruit and obedience are the right way to live and the proper response to grace; they are not the condition that places a person in Christ or keeps them there. The sharpest illustration is drawn from those who claimed the pre-resurrection power without belonging. The larger thematic warning is for us. All Scripture remains profitable for our instruction.
